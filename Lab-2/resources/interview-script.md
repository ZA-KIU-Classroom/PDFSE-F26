# How to Build an Interview Script, Start to Finish

*Concept guide for Lab 2, step C3. About 9 minutes to read. The finished Sakhli script is in `examples/sakhli-interview-script-v1.md`.*

## The idea, in plain language

An interview script is a plan for a 20-minute conversation, not a questionnaire you read aloud. You follow the person's story and may use half your questions. The script brings you back to the right things when the conversation drifts, and keeps out anything that invites a kind lie.

Every script has four parts, always in this order:

| Part | Time | Its job |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | 2 min | Say who you are, that you are not selling, and get consent |
| Warm-up | 2 min | Get them talking about their world, easily |
| Core | 12 to 15 min | The last time the problem happened, what it cost, what they tried |
| Close | 2 min | Who else to talk to; permission to come back |

Above all four sits **your prediction**, copied from the "we are wrong if" line of your problem hypothesis. You never say it aloud. It sits at the top so you check it after every interview.

## Why it matters in practice

In 1985, Coca-Cola replaced its formula with a sweeter one, New Coke, after blind taste tests with around 200,000 people that the new formula won. The launch was a disaster: people were furious about losing a drink that meant something to them. About three months later the original came back as "Coca-Cola Classic."

The research was huge and carefully run. The problem was the question. "Which sip do you prefer?" measures a sip. It says nothing about what people had been doing with the drink for years, or how they would feel about losing it. Every answer was accurate, and every answer was to the wrong question.

Your script is where you choose your questions, so it decides what you are able to learn. A perfectly run interview on a leading script produces confident, well-logged, useless data.

## Worked example: building the Sakhli script, step by step

**Step 1: Put the problem and the prediction at the top.** Keti copied the problem from DECISIONS.md and the prediction from the hypothesis: *"We are wrong if hosts do not raise the commission themselves before we mention it."*

**Step 2: Write the opening, word for word.** Nobody improvises consent well when nervous. Hers names who she is, says she is learning and not selling, admits she has nothing to show, and asks whether a teammate may take notes without using the host's name. If they say no to notes, you still talk, and the logger writes everything straight afterward, marked "from memory".

**Step 3: Write one easy warm-up.** Something they can answer at length without thinking: "Tell me about your guesthouse. How did you start hosting?" That is question 1. It relaxes them and gives you context for everything after.

**Step 4: Build the core from the skeleton.** Take the three skeleton questions from the lecture and rewrite each in your world's words:

2. "Walk me through the last booking you took, from the very first message." (the last time)
3. "Tell me about the last time a booking went wrong." (the last time, aimed at pain)
4. "What did that cost you?" (cost; push for a number)
5. "What do you use to keep track of who is arriving?" (workaround; follow with "can you show me?")

**Step 5: Add one question for each belief you did not pick.** Luka believed summer overload was the problem; Mariam believed it was answering foreign guests. Each got a neutral, past-tense question:

6. "Tell me about the busiest week you had last summer."
7. "Walk me through the last message you got from a guest abroad. What did you do?"

Neither mentions the belief. If overload or language is real, it will come up in the story.

**Step 6: Write the follow-ups you will need.** A small bank under the core for vague or polite answers: "And then what happened?", "Why was that a problem?", "When was the last time that happened?" after any "usually", "Can you show me?", "What would that let you do that you can't now?" after any feature request, and silence: count to three before speaking.

**Step 7: Write the close.** "Who else in the valley should we talk to about this?" and "Could we come back when we have something to show you?" Both ask for a small commitment; a yes is real evidence.

**Step 8: Run the audit on yourself, then swap.** Score every question:

| Score | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Green | About a specific past event, no idea in it, open | "Tell me about the last time a booking went wrong." |
| Amber | About their life, but generic or yes/no | "Do you use Booking.com?" (needs "tell me about the last time...") |
| Red | Future, hypothetical, leading, or asks them to judge an idea | "Don't you find the commission too expensive?" |

Keti's first draft had two more questions, both scored red by the auditing team and deleted: "Don't you find Booking.com's commission too expensive?" (plants her prediction in the host's mouth) and "Would you switch to your own booking page if it saved you money?" (a hypothetical future). Both protected her favorite idea, which is where reds usually hide.

**Step 9: Translate before, not during.** Interviewing in Georgian or Russian? Write the script in that language and keep the English beside it for your repo. Translating on the fly is where leading words slip in.

**Step 10: Fit it on one page.** Big text, the prediction at the top, the follow-up bank at the bottom. You will glance at it, not read it.

## Common mistakes, and how to spot them

**The hidden hypothesis.** Your belief sneaks into a question: "How much do commissions hurt you?" Compare each question to your prediction line. If a question only makes sense when your prediction is true, it is leading.

**The future tense.** "Would," "will," "could you imagine," "if there were." Search your script for those words. Each is almost always red.

**The feature menu.** "Would you rather have notifications or a calendar?" asks them to design your product. Any question that names a feature is red.

**Pitching in the opening.** "We're building an app that..." ends honest answers before the first question.
