# Writing an ICP: One Person You Could Find in a Room

*Concept guide for Lab 2, step C2. About 6 minutes to read.*

## The idea, in plain language

ICP stands for Ideal Customer Profile: a description of the one kind of person whose problem you are investigating, so specific that a stranger could pick them out of a crowd.

The word that matters is *one*. Not "students", "small businesses", or "people who travel". Those are segments: thousands of people with different problems, habits, and reasons to ignore you. An ICP is a single, sharp portrait, usually written as one person with an invented name:

> Nino, 52, runs a 6-room guesthouse in Oni. Booking.com plus a WhatsApp number on a printed card. Georgian and Russian, little English. Summer is chaos, winter is empty.

That tells you who to interview, where to find them, what language to use, when the problem shows up, and what they use today. "Hospitality business owners in Georgia" tells you none of it.

An ICP is not a marketing persona with a favorite color and a stock photo. Every line either comes from evidence (your pool, soon your interviews) or is marked as a guess you will check.

## Why it matters in practice

Last spring, one team began by describing their product as "a general handmade marketplace". Their interviews were scattered: jewelry makers, woodworkers, people who had never sold anything, established shops. Every interview pointed somewhere different, and synthesis was painful because there was no common thread to find.

After ten interviews they saw that two different people were hiding inside their segment: aspiring creators afraid to start selling, and early sellers losing track of orders in their social media messages. They narrowed to one journey for one of them, and the semester got easier: clearer interviews, a clearer MVP, a clearer pitch.

That narrowing is exactly what an ICP does, except you want it in Week 2, not Week 5. A broad segment does not protect you from missing customers. It guarantees every interview is with a slightly different person, so no pattern ever becomes visible.

## Worked example, step by step

Here is how the Sakhli team wrote Nino in about fifteen minutes.

**Step 1: Start from the strongest pool entry.** Keti's first statement was about her aunt: a 6-room guesthouse in Oni, double-booked twice in one August. They used the aunt as the seed, not as the ICP, because one person's quirks are not a profile.

**Step 2: Write the one-line sketch.** "Nino, 52, runs a 6-room guesthouse in Oni." Age, role, size, place. Each fact narrows who you look for. Six rooms, not sixty: a family operation, not a hotel.

**Step 3: Describe the situation and tools.** Booking.com, a WhatsApp number on a printed card, the phone; Georgian and Russian, little English. These facts set your interview language and show you the real competitors.

**Step 4: Name the moment the problem shows up.** "July and August evenings, when requests arrive on three channels at once." Not "sometimes" or "when busy". A moment is something you can ask about: "walk me through last August".

**Step 5: Name the cost and the workaround.** Two double-bookings last season, one refunded family; a paper notebook by the phone. Both came from Keti's observation, so the team marked them "from the pool, to be confirmed".

**Step 6: Write who they are NOT.** The step most teams skip, and the one that does the most work. Not hotels with a front desk and booking software; not hosts renting one apartment on Airbnb. Both look similar from a distance and have completely different problems. Writing them down stops you drifting into their interviews because they were easier to book.

**Step 7: Name how you will reach five of them this week.** Keti's aunt, Dato next door, three hosts in Ambrolauri through Mariam's uncle. If you cannot fill this table, your ICP may be real but unreachable this semester: go back to the Four Filters. The finished ICP is `examples/sakhli-icp.md`.

## Common mistakes, and how to spot them

**The demographic bucket.** "Students aged 18 to 25 in Georgia." Apply the room test: could a stranger find this person in a room of fifty? If the answer is "anyone young", rewrite.

**The dream customer.** Who you wish would use your product, with no link to your pool. Check the evidence line: does every major claim trace to something your team actually saw?

**Solution leakage.** "Nino needs a booking app." An ICP describes a person and their problem, never your product. Search it for product nouns: app, platform, tool, AI.

**No "not" section.** Without it, your interview list quietly fills with easier-to-reach people who are not your ICP, and your data blurs. Empty, or "nobody", means not done.

**Treating v1 as final.** Your ICP should change after your first three interviews. Unchanged by Week 4 usually means you were not listening.
