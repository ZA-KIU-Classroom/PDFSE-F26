# The Four Filters: Choosing a Problem Worth Investigating

*Concept guide for Lab 2, step C1. About 7 minutes to read.*

## The idea, in plain language

Lab 1 left you a pool of real problems, maybe nine. You have one semester, a few hours a week, and Milestone 1 in Week 4, so you can investigate one. You need a way to pick that beats "the one that sounds coolest" or "the one the loudest teammate likes".

The Four Filters are that way. Each asks one question about a problem:

1. **Do we care?** Would we still want to work on this in Week 12, when the excitement is gone and it is just work?
2. **Can we reach them?** Can we name five people who have this problem and whom we could talk to this week?
3. **Can we test it this semester?** Could a thin, working slice of a solution be live by Week 7?
4. **Is anyone paying for a workaround today?** In money, time, or effort: a spreadsheet, a notebook, a nephew who handles it.

Passing all four does not make a good business. It makes a good *investigation*: people you can reach, something true to learn this semester, and evidence the pain is real before your first question.

None of the filters asks "is this a good idea?" There is no idea yet. You are choosing what to learn about, not what to build.

## Why it matters in practice

In 2020 Quibi launched a mobile app for short, high-production video episodes, made for the spare minutes of a day. It had raised about 1.75 billion US dollars and signed famous directors and actors. It shut down roughly six months after launch.

Run Quibi through filter 4. What were people doing about the "problem" of ten spare minutes and a phone? Watching YouTube, scrolling Instagram, opening TikTok. For free, happily. Nobody was spending money, time, or effort to work around a gap, because there was no gap. A team with a notebook and five honest interviews could have seen it.

Closer to home: last spring, teams that picked their favorite idea in Week 1 without any filter found out in Week 5 or 6 that they could not reach their users, or that the problem was so mild nobody had ever tried to fix it. Changing course then meant redoing a month of work under deadline pressure. The filters move that discovery from Week 6 to today.

## Worked example, step by step

The Sakhli team had nine problems in their Lab 1 pool. Here is how they got to one.

**Step 1: Shortlist three.** Each member put one vote on the problem they would most want to investigate, then they argued for five minutes. Three rose: guesthouse bookings in Racha (Keti's), delivery route planning (Luka's), group project coordination (Mariam's). Scoring all nine carefully takes too long and invites lazy scores.

**Step 2: Score filter 2 first.** Reach is the filter most likely to kill a problem outright. Keti could name her aunt, the neighbor Dato, and three hosts in Ambrolauri through a relative: 2. Mariam could name every classmate: 2. Luka had his cousin and then nobody: 1. No zeros, so nothing died here.

**Step 3: Score filter 4 with evidence, not feelings.** Guesthouses: Keti had seen the paper notebook by the phone and the son writing English replies, 2. Delivery routes: Luka's cousin screenshots chat threads, 1. Group projects: everyone complains, nobody had ever tried a tool, a shared board, anything. Mariam argued for a 1; the team gave it 0, because complaint without effort is not a workaround.

**Step 4: Score filters 1 and 3.** Care: guesthouses 2, group projects 2, delivery 1. Testable: a booking link plus a calendar is a small slice, 2; group coordination is easy to build, 2; routing is hard to fake by Week 7, 1.

**Step 5: Total and decide.** Guesthouses 8, group projects 6, delivery 4. Guesthouses won; group projects stayed as the named runner-up.

**Step 6: Write the decision, not the solution.** "Investigate: how family guesthouse hosts in Racha get and manage bookings." Not "build a booking app". Not "commissions are too high", although Keti believed it; that belief went into the problem hypothesis, where interviews could test it. The finished scorecard is `examples/sakhli-four-filters-scorecard.md`.

## Common mistakes, and how to spot them

**Scoring without evidence.** A cell that says "2" and nothing else is a feeling. Cover the numbers and read only the evidence. If it would not convince a stranger, the score is not real.

**Confusing complaints with workarounds.** "Everyone hates X" is not filter 4. Someone who built a spreadsheet, hired a helper, or keeps a notebook is. Ask: what did they actually *do*?

**Picking a solution instead of a problem.** "Investigate: an AI study planner" has already decided the answer. Spot a technology or product noun in your pick and rewrite it as a question about people.

**Letting the runner-up die.** Write it down. If interviews kill your pick in Week 3, a scored runner-up means you restart in hours, not days.
