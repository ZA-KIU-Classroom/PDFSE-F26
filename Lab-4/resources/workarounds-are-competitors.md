# Workarounds Are Your Competitors

*Optional. Linked from Lab 4, checkpoint 3. You can finish the lab without it; your landscape will be more honest with it.*

## The concept

A competitor is anything your user does today to get the job done. That includes products, but most of the time it is something else: a paper notebook, a group chat, a spreadsheet, a cousin who helps, a habit, or simply living with the problem. Your competitive landscape seed is the list of those things, built only from what your interviewees actually named, with the log number next to each one.

The word "seed" matters. In Week 13 you will grow this into a full competitive analysis. Today you plant only what your evidence contains. A competitor you found by searching the internet but no interviewee mentioned does not go in the table. It goes in a short "nobody mentioned" list, so the team can stop arguing about it.

## Why it matters

Most teams lose to the status quo, not to a rival. A user who already has a workaround that is free, familiar, and trusted needs a strong reason to change. If you do not know what that workaround is, you will design against an imaginary enemy and be beaten by a notebook.

Slack is a well-known example of a team that understood this early. In a 2013 memo to his team, sent two weeks before Slack's preview release and later published as "We Don't Sell Saddles Here", co-founder Stewart Butterfield treated the handful of rival chat products as a side issue. The hard part, he argued, was asking teams to give up years of email habits and spend their day in an unfamiliar app. The real competitor was the habit.

The failure version shows up every semester in a quieter form: a team lists five apps they found online in their competitive table, scores each on features, and concludes the market is wide open because none of the apps does everything. Then the first user test shows the user does not use any of those apps. She uses a WhatsApp group with her sister, and it works well enough. The table was about the wrong fight.

## Worked example, step by step

The Sakhli team built their seed in fifteen minutes by reading logs, not browsers.

**Step 1: Search every log for the workaround line.** Each log from the Lab 2 template has a "Workaround" line. They copied each into a scratch list: the notebook (01, 03, 05, 07), the son writing English replies (02, 06), calling the neighbor (05).

**Step 2: Search the quotes for every named tool, person, or habit.** This found what the workaround lines missed: Booking.com (every log), WhatsApp and Instagram (08), the cousin in Shovi (05), and memory for repeat guests (04).

**Step 3: For each, write the job it does.** Not what it is, what it does for her. The notebook is "the one place every booking gets written". The son is "the English reply desk". This column is what you will have to replace.

**Step 4: For each, find the failure in their words.** The notebook: "Still, twice this season." (03). The son: "When he is in Tbilisi, nobody answers." (02). If no interviewee said how it fails, the cell says "no complaint recorded", which is itself useful: a workaround nobody complains about is a strong incumbent.

**Step 5: Name the strongest incumbent.** Not the biggest company. The one holding the job most firmly. For Sakhli it is the notebook: four logs, no complaints about the notebook itself, free, always on, trusted. That sentence changes the product: whatever Sakhli builds must beat the notebook at the moment a request arrives.

**Step 6: Park what nobody mentioned.** The team expected Airbnb and found channel-manager software online. Zero of eight hosts named either. Both went to "nobody mentioned", not the table.

## Common mistakes and how to spot them

**Googled competitors in the table.** Spot it: a row with no log number. Move it to "nobody mentioned" or delete it.

**Only software.** Spot it: every row is an app. Reread your logs for people, paper, and habits. If interviewees truly use only apps, fine, but check.

**Your opinion in the "fails at" column.** "Clunky interface" is your review, not their pain. Spot it: no quotation marks. Replace with their words or write "no complaint recorded".

**Leaving out "doing nothing".** Some users simply absorb the cost. Spot it: a log where the workaround line says "nothing" or "accepts it" and no matching row in your table. Add it; it is often the hardest competitor to beat.

**Calling the biggest company the strongest incumbent.** Spot it: your strongest incumbent sentence has no log numbers, or it names the company everyone has heard of rather than the thing your logs show people trust.

## Check yourself

1. Your team's table lists three rival apps, all from search results. Two interviewees mentioned "my spreadsheet" and one said "I just remember it." What should the table look like?
2. A workaround appears in five logs and nobody complains about it. Is that good or bad news for your product?
3. A teammate argues that the biggest app in your market must be the strongest incumbent because it has the most users. What question settles it?

*Answers.* 1: Rows for the spreadsheet (two logs) and memory (one log), with their jobs and any failure quotes; the three apps move to "nobody mentioned". 2: Both. It is a strong incumbent you must beat at a specific moment, and it tells you exactly what that moment is. If you cannot find any failure, revisit whether the problem is real. 3: "Which do our interviewees actually use today, and what did they say about it?" The logs decide, not the market size.
