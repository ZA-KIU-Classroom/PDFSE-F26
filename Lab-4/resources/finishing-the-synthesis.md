# Finishing the Synthesis

*Optional. Linked from Lab 4, checkpoints 1 and 2. You can finish the lab without it; your map will be sharper with it.*

## The concept

In Lab 3 you built a first affinity map from three or four logs. It was a draft, and drafts are allowed to be wrong. Finishing the synthesis means every real log is in, every pattern has been tested against the new logs instead of just growing, and you can say out loud which pattern is strongest and why. A finished map is not a bigger map. It is a map that has survived the interviews you ran after you first drew it.

Three moves do the work: **re-sort** (put the new quotes in without assuming the old clusters are right), **re-count** (how many different interviews sit behind each pattern, and how many raised it without being asked), and **rank** (one pattern is the strongest, and the reason is written down with log numbers).

## Why it matters

Your problem statement, your prototype, and Milestone 1's quality band all rest on the pattern you call strongest. Pick the wrong one and you build the wrong thing with total confidence.

The famous failure is New Coke. In the early 1980s Coca-Cola ran blind taste tests with roughly 200,000 people, and a sweeter formula won them. In 1985 the company replaced the original. The backlash was so fierce that the original was back on shelves within three months as Coca-Cola Classic. The taste data was real. What the company underweighted was a smaller, louder signal from its own focus groups: a minority of people were angry at the very idea of the old drink disappearing. That signal did not fit the question being asked (which tastes better?), so it sat in the leftover pile. It turned out to be the pattern that mattered.

Your version of this is the "not yet a pattern" section of your map. It is not a waste bin. It is where the next surprise is waiting for a second voice.

## Worked example, step by step

The Sakhli team's Lab 3 draft had three patterns from logs 01 to 05. Then logs 06, 07, and 08 came in.

**Step 1: Re-sort the new quotes cold.** They pulled every quote from 06, 07, and 08 onto fresh notes and grouped them before looking at the old clusters. Log 06's "a Polish family wrote on Monday, I understood on Wednesday" grouped on its own with nothing double-booking related.

**Step 2: Merge with the old map, and let clusters fight.** The new 06 note joined the English-replies cluster (with 02), turning a one-interview hunch into a pattern. Log 07's refund quote joined double-booking. Log 08's "some write on Booking, some on WhatsApp, some on Instagram" could have gone into double-booking (channels) or repeat guests (history). They asked: what is the quote actually about? She said "I don't know who is who." That is about people, not dates. It went to repeat guests.

**Step 3: Split anything carrying two ideas.** Their old double-booking cluster also held "I write everything in the notebook." That is a workaround, not a fear. They kept it in the cluster as evidence but copied it into the competitive landscape seed, where workarounds live.

**Step 4: Re-count, two numbers per pattern.**

| Pattern | Interviews | Raised unprompted |
|---|---|---|
| Hosts fear the double-book more than the commission | 01, 03, 05, 07 | 4 of 4 |
| English replies arrive too late for foreign guests | 02, 06 | 2 of 2 |
| Repeat guests get lost between apps | 04, 08 | 2 of 2 |

**Step 5: Rank and write the reason.** Double-booking wins on three counts: most interviews, a named cost (the refund in 07), and a root cause something could be built against (two channels that never sync). The reason goes at the bottom of `patterns-analysis.md` in two sentences with log numbers.

**Step 6: Check the leftover pile.** "My cousin in Shovi sends me his overflow" (05) is still alone. It stays in "not yet a pattern", and the team added a question for it to script v2.

## Common mistakes and how to spot them

**Only adding, never moving.** If no note has changed cluster since Lab 3, you did not re-sort, you appended. Spot it: ask whether any new log made you rename or merge a cluster. If the answer is no after three or more new logs, look again.

**Counting quotes instead of interviews.** Five quotes from one talkative host is one interview. Spot it: the "Interviews behind it" line has fewer numbers than the quote list has quotes.

**Ranking by how much you like the pattern.** The strongest pattern is the one with the most independent, unprompted evidence and a cost you can name. Spot it: if your "why it is strongest" sentence has no log numbers, it is a preference.

**An empty leftover pile.** At 6 to 10 interviews, some quotes should still stand alone. Spot it: if every quote fits a cluster, you probably forced a few. Pull the weakest fit out and see whether the cluster still holds.

**Promoting a prompted answer.** A quote that only appeared after you asked directly is weaker than one that came unasked. Spot it: your logs mark "only after we asked". Count those separately.

2. After four new logs, your three clusters from Lab 3 are unchanged and simply longer. What should you suspect, and what do you do?
3. Two patterns each have three interviews. One has a named cost (a lost booking, a refund). The other has strong feelings but no cost anyone mentioned. Which do you rank higher, and what do you add to your script?

*Answers.* 1: Two interviews (02 and 05). It meets the two-interview floor, but only just, and one voice dominates it; mark it as thin. 2: That you appended instead of re-sorting. Pull the new quotes out, group them cold, then merge. 3: The one with a named cost; a problem with no cost may not be worth solving. Add a past-behavior question that probes the cost of the other one ("what happened the last time...").
