# Beyond the Clickable Prototype

Not required, not graded. For teams who finish Milestone 1 early, or who want more tools before Lab 5.

## Prototypes that are not screens

**Wizard of Oz.** The tester sees a working product; behind it, a teammate does the work by hand. A "smart" reply suggestion can be a teammate typing fast in another tab. It tests whether people want the output before you build the machine that produces it. Be honest in your consent script that the test is a prototype, even if you do not explain which parts are manual until afterwards.

**Concierge.** You do the job for a few real users, openly, by hand. No pretense of software. Zappos-style. It tests whether the value is real and teaches you every step a product would need, in the order users actually need them.

**Fake door.** A button or a page for something that does not exist yet, with a "coming soon, leave your number" behind it. It measures intent with behavior instead of opinion. Use it sparingly with users you have interviewed: a fake door that disappoints someone who trusted you costs you an interviewee.

## How many testers is enough

Jakob Nielsen's well-known rule of thumb is that about five users find most of the usability problems in a single round, and that several small rounds beat one big one. The point is not the magic number. It is that you should test, fix, and test again with fresh people rather than spending your whole recruiting effort on one session list. Five is your Lab 5 target for that reason.

## RITE testing

Rapid Iterative Testing and Evaluation, a method that came out of Microsoft's game studios: when a problem is obvious after one or two sessions, fix it before the next session instead of waiting for the round to end. It trades comparability for speed. It works well for clear usability failures (a button nobody finds). It works badly for your riskiest assumption, where you need all five sessions on the same version to trust the count. Freeze the version that tests the assumption; iterate freely on everything else.

## Switching interviews

From the jobs-to-be-done tradition: interview people who recently switched from one workaround to another (from the notebook to a spreadsheet, from a cousin to an app) and reconstruct the timeline of the switch. What pushed them away from the old way, what pulled them to the new one, what made them anxious, what habit nearly kept them. Those four forces are the closest thing discovery has to a map of what your product must overcome. One or two switching interviews before Lab 5 can sharpen your riskiest assumption more than five more general interviews.

## Prompting AI prototyping tools without inventing features

Give the tool your evidence, not your vision. Paste the problem statement and the screen table from your prototype plan, list the "will not appear" items explicitly, and ask for exactly the screens in the table. Then check every generated screen against the table before showing it to anyone. The Week 4 recording walks through what happens when you skip that check.
