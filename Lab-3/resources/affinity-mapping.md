# Affinity Mapping

## The concept

Affinity mapping is how you turn a pile of interview notes into a small number of patterns you can act on. You take every note from every interview, spread them out, and move the ones that feel related next to each other. No labels at first. The groups come from the notes themselves, not from a framework you brought in the door. Once groups exist, you name each one with a sentence that states what the evidence actually says.

The method sounds simple and the discipline is where most teams fail: writing the label before the notes are grouped, or writing a label that describes a feature area instead of a finding.

## Why it matters

Your problem statement, your Milestone 1 submission, and eventually your whole product direction get built on top of whatever patterns you name this week. If the patterns are really just topics wearing pattern-shaped sentences, everything built on top of them inherits the vagueness. A team that writes "payments" as a cluster name has learned nothing an investor, a user, or a teammate didn't already know walking in. A team that writes "hosts would rather absorb a bad review than ask a guest to pay a deposit" has learned something only their interviews could have told them.

The failure story that shows up almost every semester: a team spends two hours sorting notes into categories they decided on in Week 1, before a single interview happened. The categories survive untouched because the team is pattern-matching to a plan instead of reading what's in front of them. Their "synthesis" is really just filing. The fix is mechanical: write the labels last, never first.

## Worked example, step by step

Start with ten raw notes pulled from eight Sakhli interviews:

1. Checks phone at 3am, afraid of double-booking (Int. 03)
2. Sold the same room twice in one week (Int. 01)
3. Calls the neighbor to warn her about overflow (Int. 05)
4. Keeps a paper notebook by the phone (Int. 03)
5. Son writes the English replies (Int. 02)
6. Lost a Polish family, replied two days late (Int. 06)
7. Nobody answers when son is in Tbilisi (Int. 02)
8. Missed a returning family at checkout (Int. 04)
9. Guest history lives in three different apps (Int. 08)
10. Wishes she could see who stayed before (Int. 04)

Step one: read all ten cold, no sorting yet. Resist the urge to categorize while reading, just absorb them.

Step two: move notes that feel related next to each other. Notes 1, 2, 3, and 4 all describe a fear around double-booking and the workaround of a paper notebook. They cluster. Notes 5, 6, and 7 describe language and response-time friction. They cluster separately. Notes 8, 9, and 10 describe losing track of returning guests. A third cluster.

Step three: name each cluster as a sentence, not a word. Not "Bookings," but "Hosts fear the double-book more than the commission." Not "Communication," but "English replies arrive too late for foreign guests." Not "Guests," but "Repeat guests get lost between apps."

Step four: check each cluster against the two-interview floor. All three clusters here have 2 or more interviews behind them, so all three count as patterns, not anecdotes.

## Common mistakes and how to spot them

**Writing the label before the notes are grouped.** If your cluster names were written down before you moved a single note, you built a filing system, not a synthesis. Spot it by asking: could I have written this label after Lab 1, before any interview happened? If yes, start over with the notes themselves.

**A cluster built from one talkative interviewee.** If removing a single interview collapses a cluster to zero, it is not a pattern yet. Spot it by counting unique interview numbers per cluster, not just note count.

**A label that still names a feature area.** "Calendar management" is a topic. "Hosts manually cross-check two calendars because neither one is trustworthy alone" is a pattern. Spot it by testing whether the label could describe a totally different company's users. If yes, it's still a topic.

**A misc bucket nobody will close.** Every synthesis has leftover notes that don't fit cleanly. That's fine at 3 to 6 interviews. The mistake is leaving the leftover pile untouched by Milestone 1, when it should either have grown into a real pattern or been dropped.

## Check yourself

1. Can you point to the exact interview numbers behind each of your clusters, and does every cluster have at least two?
2. If you swapped your product's name for a random competitor's, would your pattern labels still read true? If yes, they're not specific enough.
3. Did you write your cluster labels before or after you moved the notes into groups? Be honest.
