# Testing Your Prediction

## The concept

In Lab 2 you wrote a problem hypothesis and committed to a "we are wrong if" line before you talked to a single real user. That line exists so you cannot quietly redefine success after seeing the data. Testing your prediction means going back to that original line, counting what your logs actually show, and assigning one of three verdicts: confirmed, weakened, or killed. The verdict gets written into DECISIONS.md with the specific interviews that support it.

This is not the same exercise as the affinity mapping and patterns analysis you just did. Those let the evidence speak for itself, bottom-up. This step takes your top-down prediction, the one you committed to before any evidence existed, and holds it against what actually came back.

## Why it matters

Committing to a falsifiable prediction only works if you're willing to lose the bet. Teams that skip this step tend to quietly reinterpret vague early interviews as support for whatever they already believed, a kind of evidence laundering that defeats the entire point of the Evidence Rule. The "we are wrong if" line is a pre-commitment device exactly because it's much harder to talk yourself out of a specific, dated promise than a vague feeling.

The failure story: a team predicted that freelance designers would pay for a client-communication tool because "clients ask too many questions." After five interviews, only one designer mentioned client questions unprompted, and even that designer said it was mildly annoying rather than a real problem. The team's real evidence pointed toward late payments as the dominant unprompted pain. They initially logged their prediction as "weakened" rather than "killed," because weakened felt better to write down. A classmate reviewing their DECISIONS.md in a cross-team audit pushed back: one unprompted mention out of five, rated as merely annoying, is a kill, not a weakening. The team re-logged it honestly. The lesson travels: your verdict has to survive someone else reading your own evidence.

## Worked example, step by step

Sakhli's original prediction, committed in Lab 2: hosts are primarily frustrated by Booking.com's commission and would switch to a cheaper direct-booking alternative. The "we are wrong if" line: we are wrong if hosts describe the commission as routine or acceptable, and instead raise a different cost unprompted.

Step one: count unprompted mentions. Across 8 interviews, zero hosts raised commission unprompted as a pain point.

Step two: count prompted responses. All 8 were asked directly. 7 of 8 called the commission "normal" or "what everyone pays."

Step three: count what was raised unprompted instead. 6 of 8 interviews raised a different problem unprompted: double-booking fear in 4, English response time in 2.

Step four: compare to the "we are wrong if" line, written before any of this happened. The condition in that line, hosts call the commission routine and raise something else unprompted, was met almost exactly as written.

Step five: assign the verdict. Killed, not weakened. The evidence isn't mixed, it's close to unanimous in the opposite direction from the original prediction.

Step six: write the DECISIONS.md line, in the course's one format (`Wk3 · <decision> · evidence: <log files> · owner: <initials>`), with the interview files that support the verdict, not a vague "the data suggested." The `templates/prediction-trial.md` worksheet ends with this line ready to fill in.

## Common mistakes and how to spot them

**Confusing "nobody mentioned it unprompted" with "I forgot to ask."** If your script didn't include a direct question about the predicted problem until late in your interview round, your early "unprompted" counts may be artificially low for the wrong reason. Check your script version against each interview's date before counting.

**Rounding toward the verdict you want.** If 6 of 8 interviews contradict your prediction and you write "weakened" because "killed" feels like admitting defeat, you've broken the Evidence Rule's entire purpose. The verdict is not a grade on your original idea, it's a fact about the evidence.

**Writing a vague DECISIONS.md entry.** "The prediction wasn't totally right" is not a verdict. `Wk3 · Prediction "..." verdict: killed · evidence: 01-discovery/interview-logs/01-host-oni.md, 03-host-oni.md · owner: NK` is one your teammates and an auditor can actually check, and it is the only shape a DECISIONS.md line takes in this course.

**Treating "confirmed" as the only good outcome.** A confirmed prediction is not a better grade than a killed one. The milestone rubric rewards evidence-based synthesis, not having guessed correctly in Lab 2. Some of the strongest teams each semester kill their original idea in Week 3 and build something sharper on what replaces it.
