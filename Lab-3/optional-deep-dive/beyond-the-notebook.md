# Beyond the Notebook: Synthesis at Scale

Not required, not graded. This is for teams who want to go further than the lab's floor, or who are curious how synthesis works once a team has more data than a notebook and a wall of sticky notes can hold.

## Digital affinity mapping at scale

Physical sticky notes work beautifully up to about 30 to 40 notes. Past that, most teams move to a digital board (Miro, FigJam, or a plain spreadsheet with a pivot table) not because digital is inherently better, but because you need to filter, search, and re-sort without losing your place. If your team ends up with 15+ interviews by the semester's second half, consider tagging every note with interview number, date, and a rough sentiment, so you can slice the board by time (did this pattern exist in Week 3 or only appear after you changed the script?) as well as by cluster.

## Inter-rater reliability, the two-minute version

In formal qualitative research, multiple researchers independently code the same transcripts and then compare, specifically to catch the case where one person's pet theory is quietly shaping every cluster. You don't need the full academic apparatus, but the lightweight version is worth stealing: before you finalize your patterns analysis, have a teammate who didn't do the clustering look at your raw notes cold and build their own first-pass clusters. Where your clusters disagree, that disagreement is useful information, not a problem to paper over. It usually means either the pattern is genuinely ambiguous in the data, or one of you is reading in something that isn't there.

## Saturation curves

"Saturation," informally used in this lab to mean a question that's stopped producing new information, has a more rigorous cousin: plotting the number of genuinely new codes (patterns) discovered against interview number. In most qualitative research, this curve flattens hard after 12 to 20 interviews for a reasonably homogeneous population, the famous (if debated) finding behind the "12 interviews is often enough" guideline. At your scale (6 to 10 interviews this semester), you're nowhere near that flattening point, which is itself useful to know: if your patterns analysis feels "done" after 6 interviews with no new themes appearing, be suspicious that your sample is too narrow (all friends of friends, all the same age, all the same part of the valley) rather than genuinely saturated.

## When affinity mapping breaks down

Affinity mapping assumes your notes are roughly comparable in grain size and scope, a few words to a sentence, one fact each. It breaks down when a team's notes mix tiny details ("uses a blue pen") with sweeping claims ("doesn't trust technology"). If your clustering keeps feeling forced, check your note grain first before assuming your clustering skill is the problem. Oversized notes usually need to be split into two or three smaller ones before they'll cluster honestly.

## A harder root-cause tool: the fishbone

Five whys works well for a single linear chain of causation. When a pattern has multiple contributing causes that aren't simply layers of the same chain (say, double-booking is driven by both the two-calendar problem and a habit of over-committing during peak season), a fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram lets you branch into several causal threads at once instead of forcing everything into one line. Worth trying if five whys keeps feeling like it's flattening a genuinely multi-causal pattern into something artificially simple.
