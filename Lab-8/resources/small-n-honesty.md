# Small-n Honesty

## The concept

n is how many cases a number rests on. With a handful of users, your n is small, and small numbers behave badly: one person changes everything. Small-n honesty is a short rule about what you may say.

You may state a direction. "Hosts answer more slowly than guests ask." You may not state a size. "36 percent of requests convert."

In practice: report counts, not percentages. Say what the counts point toward. Then let that direction buy exactly one thing, the next experiment. A direction does not buy a strategy, a forecast, or a pitch slide.

## Why it matters

In 2015 a large group of psychologists published the results of repeating 100 studies from leading journals, as closely as they could. Nearly all of the originals had reported a clear, statistically significant effect. Only about a third of the repeats did. Many of the original studies rested on a few dozen people, and a result on a few dozen people can be an accident that looks exactly like a discovery. These were careful professionals with years of training, and small samples fooled them anyway.

You have four users, or nine, or none. The pull to say "our conversion rate is" will be strong, because a percentage sounds like knowledge. A judge at Demo Day who hears a percentage will ask "of how many?", and the answer had better not be eleven.

## Worked example, step by step

Sakhli, the evening the field week ended. Five things were said in the team chat. The team sorts them.

Step one: write each claim down exactly as it was said.

Step two: put the counts under it. "Our conversion rate is 36 percent" rests on 4 confirmations out of 11 requests.

Step three: move one case. If one more host had tapped Confirm, it would be 5 of 11, or 45 percent. If one fewer, 27. A number that swings eighteen points on two taps is not a rate. The claim becomes "4 of 11 requests were confirmed".

Step four: ask whether the direction survives the same test. "Hosts answer more slowly than guests ask." Move one case either way: still true. 11 requests in a week against a 14 hour median wait is a gap no single tap closes. That claim stands, as a direction.

Step five: look for the claim that is really a guess about a person. "Oni-2 does not want it." The count is: three requests, zero answers. The reason is not in the count. One phone call found it: she saw every request and wanted to check her paper notebook first. The claim was wrong, and no chart could have said so.

Step six: decide what the surviving direction buys. One experiment on host response time. Not a rebuilt roadmap.

## Common mistakes and how to spot them

**Percentages of tiny numbers.** Spot it: any percent sign in your readout. Replace it with "N of M".

**Averages of a handful.** A mean of six waiting times is mostly its largest value. Spot it: say the fastest, the middle one and the slowest instead.

**One user as a pattern.** "Users want dark mode," from one message. Spot it: ask how many said it, and write that number in the sentence.

**Zero as a verdict.** No opens does not mean no interest. It may mean no delivery. Spot it: check your field table before you conclude anything from a zero.

**Claiming nothing at all.** The opposite failure. Nine users is not proof, and it is not noise either. Spot it: a readout with no direction in it has wasted the week.

**Counting your own team.** Small n makes this worse: four test clicks can be half your data. Spot it: the internal filter toggle on every tile.
